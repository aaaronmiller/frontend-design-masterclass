# Layout: anatomy, compositions, first screens, nav and footer

Read when deciding what the sections are and how each is built. Everything here is
vocabulary to choose from. No page is required to have a hero, a nav of any form, a given
number of sections, or any composition below. The one layout requirement is floor item 1:
the page works from 320 to 1920 px.

Anatomy, compositions, postures and the nav and footer forms adapt Hallmark's `anatomy.md`,
`layout.md` and `craft.md` (MIT, see NOTICE). Its fixed canvas, section counts and word
budgets are left out. The numbers that remain are typical proportions, not limits.

## 1. Start from the visitor's job

What a page is built around depends on what the visitor came to do. Find the job first; it
tells you where the action lives, which sections belong to this kind of page, and how it
ends. The run of text beside a picture, a figure row, three steps and an email band is how
software pages look, and almost nothing else.

| Job | The action | Sections native to it | Ends on |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| subscribe (newsletter, waitlist, podcast) | the email field or play control, in the first screen | a sample or latest issue; who writes it; the archive as an index | a second field, or the archive itself |
| book (restaurant, class, clinic, studio) | a booking picker or the next free times, repeated beside the menu | the room or the food; the menu; a timetable; how to find it; hours and address | hours, address and booking |
| visit (shop, gallery, venue) | today's hours and the address | what is on now; what is coming, as dated rows; the shelves; getting there | visit information |
| attend (event, festival, conference) | tickets, from the first screen; the date and place are the headline | the lineup; the days as a schedule; ticket tiers; venue and travel | tickets again, or questions |
| buy (a product, a small shop) | the product with price and options | the range; the product in use; how it is made; delivery and guarantee | the offer again |
| try (app, game, desktop tool) | download or store buttons under the head | the main screen captured; features as named moments in screens; platforms | the download beside one last screen |
| sign up (software, API, developer tool) | start free, or the install command | the product at work; a code sample; pricing; questions | the same action in a short band |
| hire (studio, freelancer) | a plain contact | the work as an index of projects; one opened as a case; what the studio does | contact details |
| join (club, community, membership) | join, or "your first visit" | what a session is like; when and where; membership options | the first visit |
| listen, watch, read (band, film, publication) | the new release with its buttons | the record or issue and its contents; dates; earlier work | the dates, or more of the catalogue |
| use (dashboard, tool, internal app) | the task itself | see section 7 | nothing: an app screen does not "end" |
| read (docs, a report, an article) | finding the passage | contents; the text; examples; references | next and previous, or sources |

- The nav can carry the job's own action word: Book, Tickets, Shop, Listen, Start free.
- Proof comes in the category's own form (the menu, the lineup, the screen, the work),
  rarely as a band of numbers.
- When the brief gives none of a section's content, build it as a clearly marked sample
  the user replaces, and never state a sample as fact (floor item 5).
- A mailing-list close is native to things that recur (issues, editions, releases,
  events). Elsewhere, end on what the visitor came to do, or on the last strong section.

## 2. Compositions

Seventeen ways to build a section. They are layouts, not components: build each in the
page's own material and the brief's own content. The names match the theme files.

| Composition | What it is | Suits |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Split | a text block beside a visual; vary the ratio (5 to 7, 4 to 8, 6 to 6) | one claim with its evidence |
| Showcase | a head, then one visual across the full width, perhaps two smaller ones under it | the product, the room, the object |
| Grid | three or four items across, or two by two, each with a picture, figure or strong title | plans, products, places, dishes |
| Bento | four to six tiles in mixed spans, one clearly the lead | several unequal things that belong together |
| Rows | three to five full-width rows parted by hairlines: a title left, its line or figure right | an FAQ, a price list, a timeline, features without icons |
| Table | full width, numerals tabular, the naming column a step heavier | comparison, specs, pricing detail |
| Figures | three or four real numbers set large with labels, parted by hairlines | numbers the user actually supplied |
| Steps | three or four stages as a real ordered list, each with a title and a line | a process the visitor really goes through |
| Statement | one sentence filling most of the width in two or three lines | the page's argument, or a quotation with its source |
| Band | a change of ground, edge to edge, holding what the job wants there | the booking times, the buy box, the next date |
| Form | fields beside a head and two to four facts, one primary action | enquiry, sign-up, booking |
| Rail | a horizontal run past the container's edge, five or more items, scrollable by touch and keys | dates, issues, products, episodes |
| Pinned | one visual held sticky while short panels scroll past and change it | a product in several states |
| Switcher | one area with two to five states behind a segmented control, the first visible without script | menu by lunch and dinner, plans by month and year |
| Index | a dense scannable list of the brief's own items with their facts in columns | issues, projects, episodes, lots |
| Schedule | time against place or day, the current or next slot marked | a festival, a class week, opening hours |
| Offer | the thing for sale with what a buyer needs: picture, options, price, action, terms | a buy page, often as the first screen |

Choosing among them:

- Decide the content of a section first, then the composition that shows it best.
- Neighbouring sections with the same skeleton read as a template however good each is.
  Vary the composition, the ratio or the side.
- A dense section (grid, table, bento, index) reads better followed by a quiet one
  (statement, showcase).
- Steps are for real processes and Figures for real numbers. Together they appear on most
  generated pages; on a made page each is a choice.
- A section that is only a heading and a paragraph is usually a paragraph that belongs to
  its neighbour.
- Narrow screens: every composition stacks in reading order. A grid goes two across, then
  one. A rail stays a rail. A pinned pair unpins. A schedule becomes one column per day.
  Nothing scrolls sideways except a rail or a table that says so.

## 3. The first screen

A page does not need a hero. A document can start with its title and its first paragraph.
A tool can start with the tool. When the page does open with a statement, these are the
shapes it can take:

| Posture | Shape | Suits |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Settled | text and visual side by side, centred in the screen | a strong picture and a short claim; also the most common generated hero |
| Stage | text on the narrower side, a large visual on the other, perhaps bleeding off the edge | a product or object that deserves size |
| Banner | a short band, half the screen or less, with the first section visible below | docs, references, utilities, dashboards |
| Ledge | a tall field above, the headline resting at the base | a single image or colour doing the talking |
| Corridor | one centred narrow column, the headline larger than usual | a letter, a manifesto, a waitlist |
| Poster | a full-bleed photograph or canvas, the words on its calmest part | a place, an event, a film |
| Counter | the action is the visual: the booking times, the buy box, the subscribe field, set large | pages whose job is one action |
| Index | the content is the visual: the lineup, the menu, the list, with a small head above | pages people come to for the list |
| None | the content starts at once under a title | reports, articles, app screens |

- Open with the most characteristic thing in the subject's world (SKILL.md). The posture
  follows from what that thing is.
- With no visual, the headline can take the width and the size, or the first screen can be
  a Banner with the first section starting early.
- Size a full-height first screen in `svh` or `dvh`, not `vh`, so phone browser chrome
  does not clip it.
- Spare height is better spent on a larger visual or larger type than on more copy.

## 4. Navigation forms

Size the nav to the page's real destinations. A one-page artifact may need none.

| Form | What it is | Suits |
| --- | --- | --- |
| None | no nav; perhaps a wordmark | a single short page, a letter, a poster |
| Edge-aligned | wordmark at one edge, one or two links or an action at the other | most small sites |
| Masthead | a large centred wordmark over a rule, links beneath | publications, restaurants, anything with a name worth setting large |
| Floating pill | a compact bar inset from the edges | playful or product-led pages |
| Slab | a heavy full-width bar | poster-like and brutal directions |
| Three-part bar | wordmark, inline links, action | four or more real destinations |
| Search-led | a bar built around a search field or command pill | docs, catalogues, tools |
| Side rail | a vertical nav | docs and apps with many sections |
| In-page contents | a list of anchors, sticky or at the top | long documents and reports |

- Sticky is a choice for long pages where the action or the contents must stay in reach.
  If the bar is sticky, give it an opaque or blurred ground so content does not show
  through, and offset in-page anchors with `scroll-margin-top`.
- A highlight on the current section helps on a long page with in-page contents.
- On narrow screens, a short nav can simply stay visible. A menu button is for navs that
  do not fit, and it needs a label, `aria-expanded`, focus handling and Escape to close.
- A skip link is the first focusable element wherever a nav precedes the content.
- A theme toggle belongs in the nav only on a page that ships two modes (`color.md`).

## 5. Footer forms

| Form | What it is | Suits |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Colophon | one line or a short paragraph under a hairline: credits, address, date | letters, documents, single pages |
| Mast | the wordmark set large over the real links | brands with a name worth repeating |
| Statement | one closing sentence set large, links beneath | pages built on an argument |
| Practical | hours, address, contact, as the last useful thing | places and services |
| Index columns | grouped links | hubs with a real sitemap |
| Newsletter-first | the form leads | subscribe pages |
| None | the page simply ends | artifacts, reports, app screens |

Photo credits, legal lines and real policy links live here. Do not invent links to pages
that do not exist.

## 6. Rhythm, spacing and separation

- **A spacing scale** keeps distances related. One that works: 2, 4, 8, 12, 16, 24, 40,
  64, 96, 144 px, as `--space-*` tokens. Use `gap` between siblings.
- **Space is earned by mass.** A large gap frames a large section and reads as a hole
  under a small one. A page that looks empty wants a bigger head or visual, not a smaller
  gap. A page that looks crowded wants fewer things, not smaller ones.
- **Vary the seams.** Sections can part by whitespace, a hairline, a tinted well, or a
  change of ground. A page that uses the same separator at every seam reads as a template.
- **The surface ladder:** whitespace, then a hairline, then a tinted well, then a card.
  Climb only as far as the content needs. A card is for content that is independently
  interactive or of a different kind; a card inside a card is a containment error.
- **A container** keeps line lengths sane on wide screens: cap the content width (60 to 90
  rem is typical) and centre it, or let chosen elements bleed to the edge on purpose. Check
  at 1920 px that text has not stretched and the layout has not drifted to one corner.
- **A primary axis.** Left-biased, right-biased, top-heavy or bottom-weighted are choices.
  Everything centred is usually the absence of one.
- **Alignment.** Fewer distinct left edges read as calmer. State the alignment in the plan
  (SKILL.md asks for it) and hold it.
- **Radii:** one per level of nesting, the inner smaller than the outer by the padding
  between them.
- **Clipping:** `overflow-x: clip` on `html` and `body` prevents sideways scroll without
  breaking `position: sticky`, which `overflow: hidden` does.
- **Present a document whole.** When the page presents a document, a report or reference
  material, show all of it and organise it with layout. Tabs, accordions and "read more"
  are for content the reader chooses between, not for hiding length.

## 7. App screens and dashboards

Landing-page vocabulary mostly does not apply. Lint these with `--scope app`.

- Build around the task: what the person is here to do, what they need in view to do it,
  and what can wait behind a click.
- Density is a feature when the user is expert and returns daily. Tighter type, smaller
  controls (32 to 40 px with a larger hit area) and more per screen are right there.
- One figure that matters, one chart that argues, one table of detail beats a wall of
  equal tiles (`data.md`).
- Navigation follows the product's objects and tasks. Related screens share one nav.
- Lead a report with the line that says what matters, in bold, then the detail.
- For screens that supervise long-running work (agents, queues, jobs): treat the list as an
  inbox that empties; dim what is still working and give full emphasis to what needs a
  person; show a queued instruction as queued; make "disconnect" (reversible) look
  different from "remove" (permanent).

## 8. Print

When a page will be printed or saved as PDF (a report, an invoice, a menu): add
`@media print` that hides navigation and controls, sets a white ground and dark text,
keeps charts as SVG, and puts `break-inside: avoid` on figures, tables and cards.

## 9. Calibration

Layout habits that currently read as generated. Each is right somewhere:

- Every section wrapped in a rounded, shadowed card. Cards suit repeated items and widgets.
- A cluster of small pills near the headline ("New", "AI powered") that do nothing.
- A strip of metrics under the first screen, especially metrics nobody supplied.
- A small label above every heading.
- A row of icons whose labels restate the heading.
- Exactly three nav items whatever the site.
- Hero, three features, call to action, footer, in that order.
- Several pages in one session with the same structure in different colours.
- Dead bands: a tall first screen with an empty gap in it, or a lone narrow column in a
  wide empty section.
